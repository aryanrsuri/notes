Identity 

Paul Graham's article (Keep your identity small)[https://www.paulgraham.com/identity.html] is concise and propose a general theory:

> More generally, you can have a fruitful discussion about a topic only if it doesn't engage the identities of any of the participants.
> ...
> The most intriguing thing about this theory, if it's right, is that it explains not merely which kinds of discussions to avoid, but how to have better ideas.
> If people can't think clearly about anything that has become part of their identity, then all other things being equal, the best plan is to let as few things into your identity as possible.
> Most people reading this will already be fairly tolerant. But there is a step beyond thinking of yourself as x but tolerating y: not even to consider yourself an x.
> The more labels you have for yourself, the dumber they make you.

One facet of a more "junior" mindset (in any specialisation, I use software as my example) I think is that the code I (and nominally my agent) "produce" is a part of my identity, rather than 
an artefact of what I truly enjoy (the art of programming, or more generally problem-solving). Currently, I am in a transitionary period with my internal product: it is (rightfuly) under 
scrutiny for while doing 80% of things correct, the other 20% are more important and must be productionised. From a career point of view, this is actually a good thing: I have a mentor co-working
where I am learning so much, our rate of progress is insane, and we are solving more problems than ever before (and making a damn good product out of this v1). Why then, did it feel _so_
demoralising to get to this point, and feel as if "they don't see how much effort I put in, all the other invisible work I do to ... ". There are co-related factors surronding:
    (1) inter-personal & financial compensation
    (2) phsyce - I tend to have a larger-than-average narcissism and nervousness
    (3) sensitiity - generally, but specifically related to
    (4) identity - While the above are relevant to some degree, they are all simply a symptom, I think, of Graham's point on Identity

I identify as my product, more acutely, I identiy with every single line of code, UX bug, Backend bug, application oddity or good feature ad nauseum. This is tiring to both me and to progress; so:
    (1) "You are not your code" and 
    (2) "You are not your art". 

I produce it, I put effort into it, I coax it, but I am not _it_. This can free me. Of course, this will take time and effort, since currently the mangnifying lens is on, and 
I am getting a crash-course in what "doesn't" work about my product, but working backwards from my two real goals (in this context):
    (1) Be the best at what I do, and enjoy my self in this art
    (2) Build [my company] in whatever way I can to be a unicorn [1]

[1] I am not exaggerating here. I enjoy (and love) what I do, the company is awesome.
