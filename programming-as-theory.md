Ryle separates merely intelligent acts (fishing, recitation, etc) from intellectual activity, which is both understanding how to do a thing, but also can understand the thing with explanation, justification, teaching: a Theory. 
Naur, who believes the essential part of programming is knoweldge, uses Ryle's notion of Theory to present how important tacit knowledge is to the formulation, stewardship, and potential death of a program**; 
and that a Theory of program is being able to not just know the program (intellegent act), but rather can teach, learn, debate on parts of its justification (intellectual activity).
It is a tacit understanding about how the piece of the world it tries to solve is mapped within the program, and why this mapping is chosen over other methods.

1. The cost of not having a Theory is that disparate things such as program modification, or responding to new complexities will not be understood properly.
  1.1 A program is "dead" once all those who understand its Theory have dissolved, even if the artefact still runs.
2. The cost of program modification to the coherence of the product can be very high, if those working on it have dilute/little Theory of the program, and it is sometimes less costly to rebuild (a Theory).
3. Trying education by documentation often fails the reader, and explanation from the author generally succeeds. Why? Strictly because the author has this un-writable knoweldge about why the program function that perhaps exists between the lines of the documentation
  3.1 Thus documentation is essentially lossy to the Theory of the program

* Theory, in principles, cannot reduce to a set or sequence of rules (such as the scientific method). Since rules don't provide a surface to organise Theory.
** Here, program refers to the entire formulation, writing, execution, and project of solving a problem with software.
